The Lost Spring

A day spa filmed as a single unbroken move — through the bush, past the pools, and up over the roofs.

Role
Camera, edit, grade
Format
Continuous take, colour graded
Client
The Lost Spring
Deliverables
Six one-take laps — garden, interiors, exterior
Year
2026

The brief

The Lost Spring is a day spa built inside a dense subtropical garden — mineral pools with sculpted terracotta edges, palms and tree ferns closing overhead, a swing bridge, a café, and a cluster of red-roofed pavilions at the top of the site. The problem with filming a place like this in cuts is that cutting destroys the one thing that actually sells it: the sense that you are walking deeper into somewhere hidden.

So the films are laps. Each one is a single continuous move that enters through the bush, works its way around the water, and lifts away over the roofs to the logo. No cuts at all until the end card.

Why one take

  • Continuity sells geography. In one move the viewer learns how the paths, the pools and the café actually connect — something a montage can never teach.
  • It removes the edit as an excuse. If a corner of the garden looks untidy, there is nowhere to hide it, so the flying route itself becomes the edit.
  • It loops. A take with no cuts can run behind type on a website or play on a screen in reception without ever announcing a beginning.

Flying it

  • Route planned on foot first, then flown repeatedly — the useful laps are the ones where the speed stays constant through the tight gaps between trunks.
  • Height changes are doing the storytelling: low and slow at water level through the planting, then a climb at the end so the final frame gives up the whole property and the hills behind it.
  • Multiple complete laps delivered rather than one, so the client can pick the pacing rather than being handed a single take and told it is the film.
  • Six laps in the end, and not all of them outside: two through the garden and the pools, three through the treatment rooms and the lobby, one around the buildings. All six are below, in full.

Grading

Bush is a nightmare of greens. Under a closed canopy almost everything arrives the same flat olive, the water reads as a murky yellow-green, and the sculpted concrete goes a dead pink. The grade separates those three: it cools and deepens the foliage so the canopy has some shadow in it, warms and clears the water so the pools look inviting rather than stagnant, and holds the terracotta edging just warm enough to stay a deliberate design choice rather than a colour cast.

The other job is exposure continuity. A single take walks from deep shade into open sun and back several times, so the grade has to ride those transitions without the viewer ever seeing it happen.

The ending

Every lap finishes the same way — a climb over the pavilion roofs with the star finial in frame, the town and the hills opening up behind, and the logo resolving in the sky. It is the only moment in the film where the brand speaks, and it works because the preceding minute has earned it.

The swing bridge

The best few seconds of any lap are the ones under the swing bridge. The route drops to just above the water, the bridge deck and its cables cross the whole frame overhead, and for about a second and a half the camera is genuinely inside the architecture rather than looking at it. It is the single clearest demonstration of why the films are flown as one move: a cut to the bridge would be a shot of a bridge, while flying under it is an experience of the place. That pass is what the homepage hero video is cut from.

What it taught

A one-take is mostly rehearsal. The flying is the easy part; knowing the route well enough that you stop thinking about it is what makes the move look calm. The laps that worked were the ones flown after I had stopped looking for things to film.

It also made a strong case for restraint. There is no music cue, no whip pan, no reveal — just a steady move through a place that is already beautiful, and the discipline to let that be enough.
